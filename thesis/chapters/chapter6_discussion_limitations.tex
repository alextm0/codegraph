\chapter{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{ch:discussion}

The evaluation in \cref{ch:evaluation} quantified CodeGraph's retrieval performance; this chapter interprets what those results reveal about the nature of graph-based code retrieval. We examine three findings that extend beyond the specific system: the complementary relationship between lexical and structural signals, the conditions under which graph propagation helps or fails, and the implications of deterministic structural retrieval for AI agent design. We then assess the limitations of both the system and its evaluation.


% =============================================================================
\section{Interpretation of Results}
\label{sec:interpretation}
% =============================================================================

The 24.3 percentage point improvement over the BM25 baseline is the headline result, representing the measured value of structural propagation in code retrieval. Three interpretive findings emerge from the evaluation data.

\subsection{Lexical-Structural Complementarity}
\label{sec:complementarity}

The evaluation establishes a precise relationship between lexical and structural retrieval signals. BM25 alone reaches 49.7\% R@10; the full pipeline reaches 74.0\%. The 24.3 percentage point gap is the measured value of structural propagation, but that gap exists only because lexical matching provides the starting points. Neither signal alone is sufficient: BM25 without graph structure cannot reach files that share no vocabulary with the task description, and PPR without lexical seeds has no starting point at all.

This complementarity is not symmetric. Lexical matching is the bottleneck: when it fails to place any seed in the correct region of the graph, structural propagation has nothing to amplify. The 78 zero-recall instances are dominated by cases where seeds either land in the wrong neighborhood entirely (Semantic Gaps) or land in the right neighborhood without an explicit edge to the target (Connectivity Gaps). In both cases, the ranking function is not at fault. This asymmetry explains why the Seed-First engineering strategy, which concentrates effort on improving the quality of lexical seeds, produced the dominant share of all performance gains.

\subsection{Uniform Restart as a General Principle}
\label{sec:uniform-restart-principle}

The finding that uniform PPR restart outperforms weighted restart by 18.7 percentage points reflects a general principle in seed-based graph retrieval: when seed quality is imperfect, seed diversity matters more than seed confidence.

Weighted restart assigns more probability to seeds with higher BM25 or entity-match scores. In practice, confidence scores are unreliable indicators of correctness. A high-scoring seed may match a common identifier like \texttt{update} that appears in dozens of unrelated files. Uniform restart avoids this failure by giving every seed an equal share of restart probability. The graph topology then resolves the competition: correct seeds tend to share structural paths through common dependencies, so their PPR mass accumulates at the intersection, while isolated incorrect seeds dissipate probability locally and are overtaken.

\subsection{Bimodal Rank Distribution as a Diagnostic}
\label{sec:bimodal-methodology}

The bimodal rank distribution reported in \cref{sec:eval-diagnostics} is not only a finding about CodeGraph; it is a diagnostic methodology that other retrieval systems can apply. By plotting the rank distribution of ground-truth targets, a system designer can determine whether the bottleneck is ranking quality or reachability.

In CodeGraph's case, the near-absence of intermediate ranks demonstrated that ranking refinement had low expected value compared to seed improvement. This diagnostic directly shaped the engineering strategy and prevented wasted effort on algorithm refinement that could not have addressed the dominant failure mode.

\section{Implications for AI Coding Agents}
\label{sec:implications}
% =============================================================================

CodeGraph's results have three concrete implications for how AI coding agents interact with codebases.

\subsection{Context Quality and Reasoning Efficiency}

When retrieval is precise, the agent's context window is spent on relevant code rather than on search results that must be filtered. The improvement from 42.0\% to 74.0\% R@10 means that, for the majority of instances, the agent can begin reasoning about the bug immediately rather than requesting additional files or guessing at locations. This reduces the number of tool calls the agent must make and concentrates its limited context budget on code that matters.

\subsection{Determinism and System Transparency}

CodeGraph produces the same output for the same input. For users, this means retrieval failures can be reproduced and inspected: seeds, graph structure, and PPR scores are all available for diagnosis. For agent frameworks, determinism means retrieval results can be cached, compared across runs, and used as stable inputs to downstream reasoning.

The contrast with non-deterministic systems is instructive. When retrieval itself is non-deterministic, failures become harder to diagnose and improvements harder to validate. CodeGraph's deterministic design allowed the evaluation trajectory in \cref{sec:eval-trajectory} to attribute performance changes to specific engineering interventions with confidence.

\subsection{Tool Interface Design}

CodeGraph exposes its retrieval pipeline through two concise MCP tools, prioritizing simplicity over the expansive toolsets of other platforms. This reflects a design philosophy: a retrieval system should minimize the number of decisions the agent must make. With a single \texttt{get\_relevant\_context} call, the agent receives a ranked, token-budgeted context package. While a narrow interface limits the agent's ability to provide custom retrieval parameters, the evaluation suggests the default configuration is robust across diverse repositories and issue types.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =============================================================================

This section focuses on limitations specific to the evaluation and the dimensions of validity that affect how broadly the results should be interpreted.

\paragraph{Single-benchmark evaluation.}
All reported metrics come from SWE-bench Lite, featuring 12 well-structured open-source Python repositories. Performance on proprietary codebases or repositories with weaker structural organization may differ. Codebase structure is a strong moderator of retrieval quality, as seen in the variation between scikit-learn and Pylint.

\paragraph{File-level evaluation.}
We evaluate retrieval at the file level: an instance is a success if the gold file appears in the top-$k$ results. This does not capture cases where the relevant code spans only a few lines within a large file, or where the agent needs context from supporting files not in the gold patch.

\paragraph{Single retrieval step.}
The evaluation measures the quality of a single retrieval call. In practice, AI agents retrieve context iteratively. A system that enables effective follow-up queries might outperform one that achieves higher single-call recall but offers less structural visibility.

\paragraph{External validity.}
SWE-bench Lite covers popular open-source Python projects. Repositories with weak structural organization or inconsistent naming may produce sparser graphs and weaker seed matches. Additionally, the benchmark issues tend to include technical detail that aids lexical matching; bug reports in other settings may be less technically precise.

\paragraph{Scalability.}
PPR execution on repositories with tens of thousands of nodes requires approximately 50\,ms. For larger monorepos with millions of nodes, the GDS projection and PPR execution may require optimization strategies that are not currently implemented.
